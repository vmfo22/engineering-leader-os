# Leadership Frameworks

Additional frameworks for developing others, increasing autonomy, and leading effectively.

## Sponsorship vs Mentorship

These are different. Both matter. Don't confuse them.

### Mentorship
- **What:** Advice, guidance, sharing experience
- **Who does it:** Anyone with relevant experience
- **Where it happens:** Conversations, 1:1s
- **What it gives:** Knowledge, perspective, confidence
- **Limitation:** Doesn't directly create opportunities

### Sponsorship
- **What:** Advocating for someone in rooms they're not in
- **Who does it:** People with organizational power
- **Where it happens:** Decision-making conversations, promotions, assignments
- **What it gives:** Opportunity, visibility, career advancement
- **Limitation:** Requires spending political capital

### The Key Insight

Mentorship without sponsorship = advice without opportunity

Most people get plenty of advice. What they need is someone willing to:
- Recommend them for stretch assignments
- Advocate for their promotion
- Put their name forward for visibility
- Defend them when they're not present
- Spend political capital on their behalf

### How to Sponsor

1. **Identify who you're sponsoring** (be intentional)
2. **Put their name forward** for opportunities
3. **Advocate in talent/promotion discussions**
4. **Give them assignments that create visibility**
5. **Defend them when they make mistakes**
6. **Connect them to your network**

### Questions to Ask Yourself

- Who am I actively sponsoring right now?
- Who should I be sponsoring that I'm not?
- Am I sponsoring people who look different from me?
- When did I last spend political capital on someone?

## The Autonomy Ladder

How to increase team independence over time:

```
Level 5: They act, you're informed after
         ↑
Level 4: They decide, tell you before acting
         ↑
Level 3: They recommend, you decide
         ↑
Level 2: They research options, you direct
         ↑
Level 1: You decide, they execute
```

### Using the Ladder

**For each person and task, know what level they're at.**

| Level | Their Role | Your Role |
|-------|------------|-----------|
| 1 | Execute instructions | Decide and direct |
| 2 | Gather options and information | Make the decision |
| 3 | Analyze and recommend | Approve or redirect |
| 4 | Decide and inform | Stay aware, be available |
| 5 | Own it completely | Stay out of the way |

### Moving Up the Ladder

People should generally move up over time. Help them climb:

1. **Start where they are** - Don't assume capability
2. **Be explicit about level** - "On this project, I want you at Level 3"
3. **Increase gradually** - One level at a time
4. **Celebrate progress** - Acknowledge when they level up
5. **Adjust to context** - Same person, different levels for different tasks

### Common Mistakes

- **Dropping people at Level 5 too fast:** They fail, you blame them
- **Keeping people at Level 1 forever:** They don't grow, you bottleneck
- **Not being explicit:** Mismatched expectations
- **One size fits all:** Same person can be Level 5 on one thing, Level 2 on another

## Task-Relevant Maturity (Grove)

From Andy Grove: Match your management style to the person's maturity on the specific task.

### The Concept

**Task-Relevant Maturity (TRM) = experience + ability + confidence** on a specific task

Key insight: A senior engineer might have high TRM for architecture decisions and low TRM for people management. Adjust your style per task, not per person.

### Matching Style to TRM

| TRM Level | What They Need | Your Style |
|-----------|----------------|------------|
| **Low** | Structure, direction, frequent check-ins | Tell them what to do and how |
| **Medium** | Goals, two-way communication, support | Discuss, guide, course-correct |
| **High** | Objectives, minimal involvement | Set direction, get out of the way |

### Diagnosing TRM

**Low TRM:**
- New to the task or domain
- Uncertain or frequently asking questions
- Making mistakes that show inexperience
- Need to check in often

**Medium TRM:**
- Some experience but still developing
- Capable but benefits from guidance
- Making progress with occasional misses
- Productive discussions about approach

**High TRM:**
- Expert in this area
- Confident and competent
- Track record of success
- Often knows more than you

### Adjusting Over Time

```
New role/task → Start assuming lower TRM → Increase as demonstrated
```

Don't:
- Assume everyone is high TRM (sets them up to fail)
- Keep people at low TRM forever (infantilizing)
- Treat TRM as static (it changes with context)

## The 70/10/80 Delegation Principle

A practical framework for delegation:

> Find someone at **70%** of your capability, invest **10%** teaching them, and accept **80%** of your quality as success.

### Why This Works

**70% capability:**
- They can do the job
- They'll grow from the challenge
- They're not in over their head

**10% teaching:**
- You invest in their development
- You transfer context and knowledge
- You build capability for next time

**80% quality:**
- Good enough for most purposes
- Lets you focus on higher-leverage work
- Removes you as bottleneck

### The Math

Your time freed: 100%
Their result: 80%
Your investment: 10%
Net gain: 70%

Plus: They get better for next time.

### What NOT to Delegate (The 20%)

- Decisions that require your unique context
- High-stakes, irreversible choices
- Situations requiring your specific relationships
- Things that need your formal authority

Everything else? Delegate it.

### Protection Strategies

**Calendar Protection:**
- Block time for high-energy work
- Put boundaries around energy drains
- Schedule recovery time intentionally

**Meeting Hygiene:**
- No meeting without an agenda and purpose
- Default to shorter meetings
- Give yourself permission to skip low-value meetings

**Information Diet:**
- Limit reactive communication
- Batch email and Slack
- Protect focus time ruthlessly

**Recovery Rituals:**
- What restores you after difficult work?
- How do you transition between contexts?
- What's your daily/weekly recovery rhythm?

### Energy Allocation

Not all work is equal. Spend your best energy on your highest-leverage activities:

| Energy State | What to Do |
|--------------|------------|
| Peak energy | Strategic thinking, difficult decisions, creative work |
| Good energy | 1:1s, important meetings, complex problems |
| Low energy | Administrative tasks, routine reviews, email |
| Recovery | Rest, or nothing at all |

## Personal Board of Directors

Build a group of advisors for different aspects of your life:

### The Board

| Domain | Person | What They Provide |
|--------|--------|-------------------|
| Career strategy | | |
| Technical depth | | |
| Leadership/management | | |
| Industry knowledge | | |
| Life perspective | | |
| Truth-telling | | |

### Characteristics of Good Board Members

- Will tell you the truth, not what you want to hear
- Have relevant experience or perspective
- Actually care about your success
- Available when you need them
- Bring different viewpoints from each other

### How to Use Your Board

- Don't wait for crisis—check in regularly
- Be specific about what you need ("I need advice on X")
- Listen more than you talk
- Act on their advice (or explain why not)
- Reciprocate when you can

### Building Your Board

1. Identify gaps in your current advisors
2. Think about who you admire in each domain
3. Build the relationship over time (don't just ask for help)
4. Be explicit: "I'd value your perspective as I think through my career"

## The Eisenhower Matrix

For prioritization:

```
                    URGENT              NOT URGENT
              ┌─────────────────┬─────────────────┐
              │                 │                 │
   IMPORTANT  │    DO FIRST     │    SCHEDULE     │
              │                 │                 │
              ├─────────────────┼─────────────────┤
              │                 │                 │
NOT IMPORTANT │    DELEGATE     │    ELIMINATE    │
              │                 │                 │
              └─────────────────┴─────────────────┘
```

**Do First (Urgent + Important):** Crisis, deadlines, problems
**Schedule (Important, Not Urgent):** Strategic work, relationships, prevention
**Delegate (Urgent, Not Important):** Interruptions, some meetings, some requests
**Eliminate (Neither):** Time wasters, busy work, most email

### The Insight

Most people spend too much time in "Do First" and "Delegate" quadrants.

The highest-leverage quadrant is "Schedule"—important but not urgent:
- Strategy
- Relationship building
- Capability development
- Prevention
- Planning

If you're always firefighting, you're not doing enough "Schedule" work.

## Planning Long-Term, Ambiguous Projects

A five-step blueprint for leading the kind of high-visibility, multi-quarter initiative that's intimidating to even start. Useful as a coaching tool for EMs taking on their first large, ambiguous project.

### 1. Carve out focus time

You can't plan a complex initiative in the gaps between meetings. Dedicate uninterrupted blocks to understanding the problem space. One pattern: mornings for technical decisions and stakeholder identification, afternoons for deep learning and documentation. Protecting this time is what prevents analysis paralysis.

### 2. Understand the long-term goal

Don't try to plan three years out in detail. Internalize the overarching vision, then focus on the immediate product requirements. Two anchoring questions:
- What are the desired outcomes?
- Who are the consumers?

The answers become a North Star that guides incremental progress.

### 3. Break the problem down

Decompose the vision into discrete, manageable chunks. This surfaces dependencies and blockers, and turns one overwhelming initiative into "a bunch of smaller projects serving one single vision." Sequencing is the critical move: be explicit about what must be solved first vs. later.

### 4. Identify the simplest solution first

Resist perfectionism. Start somewhere functional, ship it, and solicit feedback to refine iteratively. The first version is allowed to be mediocre. It's a starting point, not the final answer.

### 5. Socialize the plan

Share the technical plan with stakeholders, especially owners of systems you'll touch. This uncovers hidden dependencies, brings in new collaborators, and strengthens the proposal through collective input. The step most people skip, and the one that protects you from late surprises.

### Connection to execution culture

Steps 3 and 4 are the planning-side expression of the execution norms in `high_performance_engineering.md` Section 9: *Finishing > Starting* and *bias for action on reversible decisions*. Decompose, sequence, ship a rough first cut, iterate. Step 5 is *influence over execution* in practice.

## Questions for Regular Reflection

### Weekly
- What did I learn this week about leadership?
- Who did I help grow?
- What would I do differently?

### Monthly
- Am I spending time on what matters?
- Who am I developing effectively?
- Who am I neglecting?

### Quarterly
- Am I becoming a better leader?
- What's my biggest growth area?
- Who should I add to my board?

### Annually
- What kind of leader am I becoming?
- What legacy am I building?
- What would my team say about me?
